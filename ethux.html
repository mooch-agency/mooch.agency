Contributing to Ethereum UX for humans and AI agents

Invited in by Jakub Konopka, a designer at the Ethereum Foundation, we rewrote EthUX.design's plain-language guidance for AI agents building Ethereum apps, then tested it blind before it merged.

§ 01TL;DR

Without guidance, AI agents missed the plain word in 7 of 9 runs. With the guidance we rewrote, 1 of 9.

EthUX.design turns more than 32,000 Ethereum user reports into UX guidance that AI coding agents read before they build. Its founder, Jakub Konopka, invited us into the small group shaping it. We rewrote its plain-language word list in two pull requests, and both merged. Before the second, Jakub asked for proof it wouldn't make agents worse. So we built a blind test across three AI models from Anthropic and OpenAI, and he merged on the results.

§ 02The brief

Language is where Ethereum UX breaks first. A new user meets “gas”, “slippage” and “approve” before they've done anything at all. AI agents now write a growing share of that copy, and an agent uses whatever words it's given. Fix the guidance an agent reads and the fix reaches every screen it builds.

That's the idea behind EthUX.design. Jakub mapped 38 of Ethereum's UX problems from those reports and published the fixes as skills, instruction files an agent reads before it writes code. The aim: UX best practice for teams building at the frontier of Ethereum and AI, and for the agents doing the building.

We'd worked with Jakub before, rewriting the core pages of ethereum.org for the Ethereum Foundation. This time we set the brief ourselves: a plain-language standard for agents that turns hard jargon into everyday words, brings the reading level down and spells out what to avoid.

As a builder using an AI agent on an Ethereum app
I want it to write copy a first-time user understands
so that people don't give up at the first unfamiliar word

§ 03The work

1. Ship the safe changes first

Our first glossary renamed a lot, on purpose, to invite challenge. Sasha, a UX researcher in the group, took us up on it. In her research, people didn't struggle with “token”, and NFTs are tokens too, so a blanket swap to “coin” would be wrong. Every rename, she argued, has to be worth the cost to everyone who already knows the old word.

So our first pull request held back everything contested and shipped the eight safest renames. Allowance becomes “Spending limit”, Tx hash becomes “Transaction ID”, Testnet becomes “Practice network”. Jakub merged it in four days.

2. Rebuild the guidance for agents

Then we went back for the hard words. A two-column swap table can only tell an agent to replace a term, and “token” needs a different instruction: keep it and explain it. So we rebuilt the table around the action an agent should take. Replace the term, keep it and explain it, or leave it out of the beginner view.

Jakub's review sharpened it: drop the fee units Gwei and Wei, which a beginner never acts on, drop ERC-20, and keep “smart contract” mapped to “App”. We took all three.

Direction is right and the split is worth keeping. Jakub Konopka, EthUX.design, review of pull request #10

3. Prove it before it merges

Jakub wanted evidence before he'd merge: regression tests showing the change wouldn't make the models write worse copy. We built them on his own with-and-without method, using current models:

  • Three moments a first-time user meets: letting a swap app spend their USDC and later taking that away, a swap that fails because the price moved, and moving funds to a cheaper network.
  • Three models: Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Opus 5.5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.3 Codex.
  • Three setups: no guidance, the guidance as it stood, and the guidance with our change. That makes 27 runs.
  • A separate model graded every output against five yes-or-no checks, blind to which model or setup wrote it.

§ 04What we found

The existing guidance cut plain-word misses from 7 in 9 runs to 3. Our rebuilt table cut them to 1.

Guidance made the difference on one check above all: whether the copy used the plain term, like “Remove permission” for revoke or “Price change tolerance” for slippage. Across all five checks, the models scored 39 out of 45 with guidance and 35 without.

Both contributions now ship in the guidance EthUX.design serves to every agent that reads it. And the method travels: a small, blind, repeatable test that tells a maintainer whether a change to agent guidance helps or hurts, before it merges.

Thanks to Jakub, whose review made the second pull request better and whose request for proof produced the test, and to Sasha, whose research-backed pushback decided what went into the first.
