Case Study

Plain words for agents

Two small changes to an open-source Ethereum UX guide, and a blind test showing AI agents write clearer copy when they read it.

§ 01TL;DR

With the guidance, AI models scored 39 out of 45. Without it, 35.

EthUX.design maps where Ethereum trips people up, drawn from more than 32,000 user reports, and publishes the fixes as skills: instruction files that AI coding agents read before they build. We made two contributions to its plain-language word list, and both were merged. Before merging the second, Jakub, who maintains the project, asked for proof it wouldn't make agents worse. So we tested it: 27 runs across three models, graded blind. The whole gap came down to one habit, reaching for the plain word.

§ 02The brief

Language is where Ethereum UX breaks first. A new user meets “gas”, “slippage” and “approve” before they've done anything at all. More and more of that copy is now written by AI agents, and an agent uses whatever words it has been given.

EthUX.design's onboarding skill carries a table of crypto terms and the plain words to use instead. In July we shared a draft glossary in the EthUX.design community chat. Jakub said go for it, and the group agreed that pull requests on GitHub were the way to contribute.

As a builder using an AI agent on an Ethereum app
I want it to write copy a first-time user understands
so that people don't give up at the first unfamiliar word

§ 03The work

First pull request: the easy eight

Our draft renamed a lot. Sasha, a UX researcher in the group, pushed back on several of the renames. Her point on “token” stuck: swapping it for “coin” everywhere is wrong, because NFTs are tokens too, and the people in her research had never struggled with the word. She also argued that any rename has to be weighed against everyone who already knows the old word, and that the list should say how its terms were chosen.

So we held back everything contested (token, Gwei and Wei, ERC-20, bridge) and sent only the eight least contested. Allowance becomes “Spending limit”, Tx hash becomes “Transaction ID”, Testnet becomes “Practice network”, and so on. One file, ten added lines. Jakub merged it four days later, on 8 August.

Second pull request: the contested terms

The second pull request came back for the words we'd left out. A two-column table can only tell an agent to swap a word, and “token” needs a different instruction: keep the word and explain it. So we grouped the table by the action to take. Replace the term by default, keep it and explain it in place, or leave it out of the beginner view entirely.

Jakub's review asked for three changes. Drop Gwei and Wei, because a beginner never has to act on a fee unit. Drop ERC-20, because our own note said to hide it from everyday users, which made it a row that, in his words, “argues against its own existence”. And keep “smart contract” mapped to “App”, naming the app instead wherever a screen needs both. He was right on all three, and we took them.

He also caught a mistake. Our description said one table was unchanged when we had cut it from 20 rows to 17. We rewrote it with the counts row by row and the reason behind each change, which is now how we write every pull request description.

Direction is right and the split is worth keeping. Jakub, EthUX.design, review of pull request #10

The test

Then Jakub asked for regression tests before merging: checks that the change wouldn't make any model write worse copy. He had tested earlier changes by running scenarios with and without the skill, so we used his method on current models.

  • Three scenarios a first-time user meets: letting a swap app spend their USDC and later taking that away, a swap that fails because the price moved, and moving funds to a cheaper network.
  • Three models: Claude Haiku 4.5, Claude Opus 5.5 and OpenAI's GPT-5.3 Codex.
  • Three setups: no guidance, the guidance as it stood, and the guidance with our change. That makes 27 runs.
  • A separate model graded every output against five yes-or-no checks, without knowing which model or setup wrote it.

§ 04What we found

The gap came down to one habit: using the plain word.

With the guidance, the models scored 39 out of 45, both before and after our change. Without it, they scored 35. Between no guidance and the guidance as it stood, all four of those points came from a single check: whether the copy used the plain term, like “Remove permission” for revoke or “Price change tolerance” for slippage. With no guidance, 7 of 9 runs missed it. With the old table, 3 of 9. With ours, 1 of 9.

Points out of 15 per model: three scenarios, five checks each. One run per combination.

It wasn't clean. On the pass rule we'd agreed, our change failed by a point: Opus scored 15 before it and 14 after. That point was the grader's slip. It passed a sentence in one output and failed the identical sentence in the other. Haiku also did better with no guidance on the network scenario than with either table, and with ours it showed users the word “calldata”. With one run per combination, a point either way can be chance.

We sent Jakub the results as they stood, flaws included, with no reruns. He merged on 2 October.

§ 05Impact

35/45Without it
2/2Pull requests merged

Both contributions are now in the guidance EthUX.design serves to every agent that reads it. The method matters as much as the words: a small, blind, repeatable test that tells a maintainer whether a change to agent guidance helps or hurts before it merges.

Thanks to Jakub, whose review made the second pull request better and whose request for proof produced the test, and to Sasha, whose research-backed pushback decided what went into the first.
